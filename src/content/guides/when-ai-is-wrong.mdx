## Asking again doesn't fix it

When an answer looks off, most of us ask again, or ask "are you sure?", until it looks right. That isn't checking. The tool will happily agree with itself, or swap one confident answer for another, and it knows no more about what's true the second time. Asking again tells you how the answer *sounds*. Only the source tells you whether it's *right*.

Checking every answer by hand against the source does work, but it doesn't scale: the more you use AI, the more answers there are to check. The better move is to build the check into how you work. Below are the five mistakes that check is looking for, each with a quick check you can do in 30 seconds, and then how to build the check in.

## Five mistakes and the check for each

### Invented numbers or facts

The answer states something, a policy, a date, a figure, that isn't in what you gave it, or isn't true at all. It reads exactly like the true parts around it.

**The 30-second check:** for each claim you'll rely on, find the sentence in your source that says it. No sentence, no claim.

**It has happened:**
- In 2022, the chatbot on Air Canada's website suggested a customer could apply for a bereavement fare after travelling. The airline did not allow that. In 2024 a Canadian tribunal held that Air Canada was responsible for what its chatbot said and ordered it to pay the customer. ([Moffatt v. Air Canada, Civil Resolution Tribunal decision](https://decisions.civilresolutionbc.ca/crt/crtd/en/525448/1/document.do))
- In February 2023, an ad Google posted for its new chatbot, Bard, showed it saying the James Webb Space Telescope took the very first pictures of planets outside our solar system. Those first pictures were taken in 2004 by a different telescope. ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2023/02/09/1155650909/google-chatbot--error-bard-shares))

### Dropped rows

You ask for every row that matches, and some are missing. The rows it does list are correct, and nothing in the answer says it skipped any, so it looks complete.

**The 30-second check:** count the matching rows yourself, with a filter or a sort in the sheet, and compare the count with the answer.

I haven't found a documented public case of this one to link, so the game has the only example on this page. It's still worth the check: a list with a row missing looks exactly like a full list.

### Summaries of things it didn't read

A summary that sounds right but says something the document doesn't, often because it leans on the start and misses a line further down: the decision, the exception, the "but".

**The 30-second check:** for each line of the summary, find the line in the original it came from. Read the decisions and the end of the document yourself.

**It has happened:**
- In December 2024, Apple's iPhone feature that summarises notifications made it look as if BBC News had reported that a man arrested in a high-profile US murder case had shot himself. He had not. The BBC complained to Apple. ([BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cd0elzk24dno))

### Made-up sources

The answer points to a case, a book, a law or a page that doesn't exist. Made-up sources look like real ones: a name, a year, a number.

**The 30-second check:** open every source the answer names. If you can't find it, take it out.

**It has happened:**
- In 2023, a US federal judge sanctioned two lawyers and their firm for submitting court opinions that did not exist, with fake quotes and citations created by ChatGPT, and for standing by them after the court questioned them. ([Mata v. Avianca, opinion and order on sanctions](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368.54.0_1.pdf))
- In May 2025, NPR reported on a syndicated summer reading list that ran in the Chicago Sun-Times and other papers. Only five of its 15 books were real. The writer said it was partly generated by AI. ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/05/20/nx-s1-5405022/fake-summer-reading-list-ai))

### Wrong arithmetic

Totals, averages and percentages that are close to right, which is what makes them easy to believe.

**The 30-second check:** redo any number that matters with a calculator or the sheet's own SUM. Don't ask the chat to check its own maths.

**It has happened:**
- An AI-written CNET article on compound interest said that if you put $10,000 in an account earning 3% a year, you'd earn $10,300 in the first year. You'd earn $300. By the time Engadget reported it, in January 2023, the article had been corrected. ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/cnet-reviewing-ai-written-articles-serious-errors-113041405.html))

## Spot the mistake

Five rounds, one for each mistake above. Each answer has one part that's wrong. Compare it with the source and tap the part you think is wrong. Nothing you tap is saved or sent anywhere.

## Build the check in

People who use AI a lot don't hunt for mistakes after the fact. They set things up so mistakes get caught by default, as part of the work.

I don't check every answer by hand any more. Over time I've set things up so the AI has to show where each claim came from, does its sums somewhere I can see them, and gets checked by a second pass before I trust it. None of that is magic, and you can start with the habits below today. Getting it to run on your own work takes some setting up, and that's what we do in the hands-on training.

Habits you can start today, with no setup:

- **Ask for the line behind each claim.** Ask the AI to quote the line in your source behind every number or claim, and to say what it couldn't find.
- **Do the sums in a spreadsheet,** not in the chat.
- **Get a second pass.** Open a fresh chat, or a second tool, and ask it to check the first answer against the source.
- **Keep a check you paste in.** For a task you do often, keep a short check and paste it in with every task of that kind. For example: "Quote the line behind each number. List anything you couldn't find."

## The checklist

Before you use an answer from AI:

- [ ] **Facts:** can I point to where each claim I'm relying on comes from?
- [ ] **Lists:** does the count match what I get when I filter the source myself?
- [ ] **Summaries:** did I read the decisions and the end of the original?
- [ ] **Sources:** did I open every link, book, case or law it names?
- [ ] **Numbers:** did I redo the totals that matter with a calculator or the sheet?
- [ ] **Asking again:** did I check against the source, not just ask the chat if it's sure?

## When not to use AI

- **When you can't check the answer and being wrong would hurt.** Legal, medical and money questions are the usual ones. Ask a person who knows and can be held to it.
- **When the exact number is the point and you already have the sheet.** Let the sheet add it up.
- **When the only way to ask is to paste something private.** See [Never paste these](/ai-guides/never-paste/) first.
- **When checking would take longer than doing it yourself.** A short reply you could write in a minute doesn't need a draft you then have to check.
