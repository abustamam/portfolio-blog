## You type, it writes back

When people say "AI" these days, they almost always mean one thing: a program you type a message to, and it writes a reply in ordinary sentences. That's all I mean by "AI" in these guides, and it's where a beginner starts: you type, it writes back. It's not a robot and it's not a person. Many apps can now also do things for you, such as search the web, read a file you give them, or take several steps on a task. An <Term>agent</Term> is an AI set up to carry out several steps on its own.

The app or website where you do the typing is a chat app. You'll also hear "chatbot" or "AI assistant". They mean the same thing. ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot and Claude are all chat apps. One conversation with it, from your first message to your last, is called a chat.

What you get back is the answer, also called the response. You can ask for a plain-words version of a letter, a list of dinners, or a first draft of an email, and it writes something in a few seconds.

## How it comes up with an answer

Think of it like the predictive text on your phone's keyboard, on steroids. It does one thing, over and over. It looks at the words so far and works out which word is likely to come next. It adds that word, then looks at the longer text and does it again, until it reaches a sensible stopping point.

It learned which words tend to follow which from a very large amount of writing, called its <Term>training data</Term>: books, websites, manuals, forum posts. It didn't memorize a page to look up later. It learned patterns, the way you know that "I left my umbrella at home, so I got..." is probably going to end with "wet".

Three things to notice:

- **Every word is a guess about what usually comes next.** It isn't checking whether the sentence is true. It has no idea whether you actually got wet.
- **A likely word isn't the same as a correct word.** Usually the two line up, which is why it's so useful. Sometimes they don't.
- **It doesn't always take the top word.** On the fifth word it picked "home" over "to". It's built to pick with a bit of chance on purpose, so its writing doesn't sound the same every time. That's why you can ask the same question twice and get two different answers. The technical words are "stochastic" (involving chance) and "temperature" (the setting for how much chance).

## Not a search engine

A search engine finds pages that someone wrote and shows you a list. A chat app writes a new answer, one word at a time, as above. It isn't opening a page and copying from it.

Both can be useful. The difference is what you can check. With search, you can open the page and see who wrote it. With a chat app, there's often no page behind the sentence at all. It sounds as sure of itself when it is wrong as when it is right, because it's writing what usually comes next, not looking up what's true.

When it states something false as if it were true, such as an invented number, quote or source, that is called a <Term>hallucination</Term>. [When AI gets it wrong](/ai-guides/when-ai-is-wrong/) covers the five mistakes I see most and the 30-second check for each.

Some apps can also search the web when you ask. Then the answer is built from pages it just read, and you can open them. If your app doesn't show its pages, treat the answer as unchecked.

## Not a person

It feels like talking to someone, so it is easy to treat it that way. A few things are different.

- **It has no opinions of its own.** If you ask what it thinks, it writes what a thoughtful person might say. Ask it again with different wording and you may get the opposite.
- **It doesn't know you.** By default it has no <Term>memory</Term> of you or of your earlier chats. A new chat starts without your earlier chats in view, and anything it needs to know, you have to tell it. Some apps can keep a few saved facts about you; [Does it remember me?](/ai-guides/does-it-remember/) covers that.
- **It sounds sure either way.** A right answer and a wrong one come out in the same calm tone. Tone tells you nothing about whether it is right.
- **It is usually agreeable.** If you push back, it will often change its answer, even when the first one was fine. Push back and you'll often hear "You're absolutely right!", even when it was right the first time.

You decide what's true and what to do with the answer.

## Three words you will keep seeing

- **Chat.** One conversation, as above. Start a new chat and the earlier one is no longer in view.
- **Prompt.** What you type to it: your question or instructions, and anything you paste in with them. A clear prompt gets a better answer. There is more on that in [how to ask](/ai-guides/prompt/).
- **Model.** The part that writes the answers. One app usually offers a few models, from small and quick to large and careful. If you see "<Term>LLM</Term>" or "large language model", read it as "model". [Which model to pick in the menu](/ai-guides/which-model/) explains the sizes.

## What people use it for in an ordinary week

I keep these small, because small is where it helps most:

- **A week of dinners for four from what's in the fridge.** You list what you have and who is eating. If your app takes photos, you can photograph the inside of your fridge instead of listing it ([Photos, files, voice and your phone](/ai-guides/photos-files-voice/) covers how). It suggests meals and a shopping list for what is missing. You check that nobody on the list is allergic to anything it suggested.
- **A plain-words explanation of a letter from an insurer.** You paste in the letter, with your name and policy number taken out first. It tells you what the letter is asking you to do and by when. You check the date and the amount against the letter itself.
- **A first draft of a note to a child's teacher.** You say what happened and how you want to sound. It gives you something to edit, which is easier than staring at a blank page.

In each one, it does the first pass and you do the check.
